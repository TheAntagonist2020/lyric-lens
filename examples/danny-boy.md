## "Danny Boy," words by Frederic Weatherly (set to the "Londonderry Air," 1913)

*I counted the 16 lyric lines you pasted, starting at L1. Blank lines are not counted. L1–8 are the first verse and L9–16 the second.*

### Thesis
The song is about one person who goes and one who stays. Verse 1 sets this up: the speaker is the fixed point ("I must bide," L4) and Danny is the one who moves. Verse 2 then takes "staying" literally, and the speaker's promise to "be here" (L7) becomes a grave. By the last line the direction of travel has flipped. Danny began by going *away* and *down* the mountain (L2). In the end he is the one who will have to come *down* to the speaker (L16).

### Motifs and metaphors
- **"Here" becomes the grave** (L4, L7, L11). "I'll be here in sunshine or in shadow" (L7) sounds like a promise to wait through good times and bad. After L10, "shadow" also means *dead*, and verse 2 cashes that meaning in: "the place where I am lying" (L11) is the "here" of L7. The first verse already holds the burial in disguise. [Inference]
- **Snow and warmth** (L6, L14). "Hushed and white with snow" (L6) is offered as one season to come back in. Hushed and white is also how a grave looks. When the grave turns "warmer, sweeter" (L14), the song is answering its own cold image. Love works as a heat source that reaches through the ground. [Inference]
- **The seasons go in a circle, and the hoped-for one never comes** (L3, L5, L9). Danny leaves as "the roses [are] falling" (L3). The speaker asks him back "when summer's in the meadow" (L5). But verse 2 imagines him coming back "when ... all the flowers are dying" (L9), the same dying season he left in. The summer homecoming the speaker asked for is quietly replaced by an autumn return to a grave. [Text for the seasons; Inference for what it means]
- **Calling and hearing** (L1, L13, L15). The song opens with a sound that takes Danny away: the pipes calling (L1). It closes with a sound that brings him back: his voice telling the speaker he loves them (L15), which the speaker says they "shall hear" (L13). The pipes call him *out*. His own voice is what finally calls him *in*. [Inference]
- **Vertical movement** (L2, L13, L15, L16). The pipes call "down the mountain side" (L2). At the grave Danny is "above" (L13), and he must "bend" (L15) and "come to me" (L16). Each move in verse 2 is downward, toward the speaker. [Text]

### Voice, tone, and perspective
- **The speaker has no name and no gender in the text.** Only Danny is named. That is part of why the song has been sung by mothers, fathers, lovers, and at funerals. [Text]
- **Commands turn into predictions.** Verse 1 asks: "come ye back" (L5). Verse 2 starts with one conditional, "If I am dead" (L10), and after that everything is in the future tense: "Ye'll come," "kneel," "I shall hear," "you will bend" (L11–15). The speaker stops asking Danny to return and starts scripting exactly what he will do. Tenderness and control sit very close together here. [Text; Inference on the effect]
- **The person who stays is the one who expects to die.** Danny is the one heading toward pipes, mountains, and maybe war. Yet the line "as dead I well may be" (L10) belongs to the speaker who stays home. That fits an older speaker (a parent), or a long absence (emigration) more than a short campaign. [Inference]
- **The love is one-sided until after death.** The speaker says "I love you so" (L8). Danny's "you love me" (L15) exists only in a future the speaker imagines, spoken to a grave. In the song as written, the speaker never hears it alive. [Text; Inference]

### Sound and form
- **The stammer.** "The pipes, the pipes" (L1), "it's you, it's you" (L4), "Oh Danny boy, oh Danny boy" (L8): each line of the first verse that carries a lot of feeling repeats itself, as if the speaker can't get past the phrase. Verse 2 has no doubled phrases. Its feeling comes out as calm, ordered syntax: and... and... and... (L12–16). Grief stammers in the first verse and turns into ritual in the second. [Text; Inference]
- **The second verse's rhymes close in on "me."** Verse 1's masculine rhymes are two different sounds: *side / bide*, *snow / so*. In verse 2 all four are the same sound: *be / me / be / me* (L10, L12, L14, L16). The verse keeps returning to the speaker's own self. The feminine rhyme *above me / love me* (L13/L15) also pairs where Danny is standing with what he says. [Text]
- **"It's you ... must go" / "It's I'll be here"** (L4, L7). This is the Hiberno-English cleft construction ("it's X [that] ..."). It puts the stress on the person: *you* go, *I* stay. It also sets the speaker's voice in Ireland, whoever wrote the words. [Text]
- **Where the top note falls.** You said the climax lands on "I love you so" (L8). If verse 2 reuses the melody, then the same musical peak in the parallel line carries the move toward "until you come to me" (L16). The song's highest point shifts from a declaration to a summons. I haven't checked how the syllables fit the tune in the second verse, so this is [Inference]. Does the climax land there in the version you hear?

### The ending
**L16, "until you come to me"**
- **Reading A: Danny's own death.** The speaker will rest until Danny dies and joins them. "Sleep" is the old word for the sleep of the dead, and "to me" points down into the grave, where the "above me" of L13 has already placed him. Support: L10, L14, L16.
- **Reading B: his next visit.** The speaker sleeps peacefully between Danny's visits to the grave, and each "coming" brings back the warmth of L14. Support: in L5, L9, and L11, "come" has always meant coming *back to this place*.
- **Reading C, a lesser one: Christian resurrection.** "Sleep ... until" echoes the language of the dead waiting for the resurrection, and the Ave (L12) gives the song a Catholic frame. But the one who "comes" is Danny, not Christ, which weakens this reading.
- **The text leans toward A, though B stays possible.** Here is why. Every earlier "come" has a place as its goal (back, here, the place where I am lying). L16 is the first one whose goal is a *person*, "to me," and the speaker is no longer a place you can visit. The verb has kept its form and changed its meaning, and that change seems deliberate: the last homecoming the song can imagine is a death. [Inference]

### Context check
- Weatherly, an English lawyer and lyricist, wrote the words in Bath in 1910 for a different tune. In 1913 he reworked them to fit the "Londonderry Air." [(Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Boy), tier B/C [Context]
- Accounts differ on how he got the tune. Most say his sister-in-law Margaret ("Jess") Weatherly sent it or sang it to him, in 1912 or 1913. One version says she set the words to the tune herself. [(Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Boy), [(Coleraine Times)](https://colerainetimes.co.uk/arts-and-culture/new-danny-boy-revelations-uncovered-by-family-research-2695062), tier C [Context]. *If the text was refitted to the tune, the doubled phrases (L1, L4, L8) may partly come from filling out the melody. That doesn't cancel what they do in the song, but it is worth knowing.*
- I found no statement from Weatherly about who the speaker is, or whether Danny leaves for war or emigrates. Sources say his intent is unknown. The only documented comment I found: in 1928 he suggested **the second verse** as a fitting requiem for Ellen Terry. [(Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Boy), [(WRTI)](https://www.wrti.org/arts-desk/2021-03-16/the-mysteries-behind-the-beloved-irish-ballad-danny-boy), tier B/C [Context]. **This fits the reading above.** Weatherly himself treated verse 2 as a funeral text that works without Danny. In other words, the grave can serve as the song's real subject.
- The 1918 sheet music printed an alternative "Eily Dear" lyric for male singers. That implies the publisher assumed the default speaker was a woman, though it may not reflect Weatherly's intent. [(Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Boy), tier C [Context]. The text itself stays ungendered.
- Some commentators note that war pipes calling men to arms is an image tied more to Scotland than to Ireland. [(WRTI)](https://www.wrti.org/arts-desk/2021-03-16/the-mysteries-behind-the-beloved-irish-ballad-danny-boy), tier C. The text never says "war." It only says the pipes are calling.

### Open questions
1. **Why "ye" and then "you"?** Danny is "ye" in all the come-back lines (L5, L9, L11) and "you" at the grave (L13, L15, L16). It could be meter. It could also be a shift in intimacy once the speaker is dead.
2. **Is the speaker's script for Danny (L11–16) loving, or a quiet way to bind him?** It tells him where to kneel, what to pray, and what to say.
3. **Does the song need Danny to come back at all?** Weatherly's Ellen Terry suggestion implies verse 2 works without verse 1, which would make Danny a pretext for an elegy the speaker writes for themself.
